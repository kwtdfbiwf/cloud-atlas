The lid on the weather

This is the tropopause. Above it, in the stratosphere, the air warms with height and clouds have almost nothing to grow from. Thunderstorm anvils flatten against this ceiling. Every cloud you just met lives below you now.

Typical cloud-base ranges overlap and vary by latitude and season. The altimeter shows your viewpoint, not each cloud’s base.

High clouds · 5,000 – 13,000 m

Made of ice

Into the ice

The air is thin and colder than −30 °C. Clouds above this line are built almost entirely from ice crystals, which is why they look so fine, bright and fibrous. Their names all start with cirro-, from the Latin for a curl of hair.

Mid-level clouds · 2,000 – 7,000 m

The alto layer

Past the freezing level

Air cools about 6.5 °C for every kilometre you climb. Up here, cloud droplets are often supercooled — still liquid well below 0 °C, waiting for a speck of dust or ice to freeze onto.

Low clouds · ground – 2,000 m

Close to home

Lifting off

Every cloud begins the same way. Warm, moist air rises, expands and cools until its water vapour condenses onto tiny specks of salt, dust or smoke. Billions of those droplets together are what you see as a cloud.
